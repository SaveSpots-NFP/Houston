# Deliverable 1: Houston City Profile and Harm Reduction Landscape

**Scope:** Houston service system and Harris County mortality/surveillance geography. The two geographies are not interchangeable: deaths and community programs discussed below are generally countywide unless identified as Houston-city based. **Sources checked:** 2026-10-17; the mortality extract in [overdose-burden-numbers.md](overdose-burden-numbers.md) was last retrieved 2026-09-15. **Narrative word count:** approximately 2,140, excluding source notes.

## Overdose burden

Harris County's local mortality surveillance shows a sharp recent decline in recorded substance-involved deaths, but the newest observations remain provisional. The Harris County Public Health (HCPH) Substance Use Data Hub, using Harris County Institute of Forensic Sciences (HCIFS) data, reports 1,159 deaths in 2021, 1,177 in 2022, 1,126 in 2023, 970 in 2024, and 776 in 2025. The 2025 value is 33.0% below 2021 and 34.1% below the 2022 peak. This is a **published administrative surveillance series**, not an independently measured research count; HCPH says late toxicology can delay reporting and free-text substance entry can yield inconsistent or misclassified categories. A declining series cannot by itself establish that risk has receded or outreach supply can be reduced. Naloxone planning should retain the county series as a local burden signal while checking revisions and comparing it with Texas death-certificate counts, which use a different definition.

Fentanyl remains a major, but not exclusive, component of the Harris County profile. HCPH's public IFS CDC Drug Group table reports fentanyl-involved deaths of 482, 583, 570, 395, and 219 for 2021-2025. Dividing these counts by the corresponding all-substance totals yields published-data-derived shares of 41.6%, 49.5%, 50.6%, 40.7%, and 28.2%. Substance categories overlap, so these are involvement shares, not additive partitions; the newest decline is especially sensitive to pending toxicology. The county's locally observed fentanyl contribution supports distributing naloxone beyond people who identify opioids as their drug of use, including at mixed-substance and stimulant-serving locations, and keeping the fentanyl series separate from mutually non-exclusive categories.

A county polysubstance share is not available from the public products reviewed. HCPH's drug-group table does not show distinct substances per death or a validated multiple-substance flag; adding categories would double-count. A statewide estimate cannot stand in for Harris County. HCPH/HCIFS should be asked for a privacy-protected annual aggregate with a stated definition; until received, the gap supports broad-spectrum overdose education and naloxone rather than a single-drug message.

Harris County's public Houston ESSENCE feed recorded 8,972 all-drug overdose healthcare visits in 2021, 9,141 in 2022, 12,187 in 2023, 11,606 in 2024, and 9,709 in 2025. These are **published monthly visit totals summed by year** from HCPH's public feature service, which describes emergency-department, freestanding emergency-center, and urgent-care syndromic surveillance; they are not unique people or confirmed overdose deaths. The 2025 total is 8.2% above 2021 but 20.3% below the 2023 peak, indicating that fatal and nonfatal series have not moved in lockstep. Because visits depend on care-seeking and ESSENCE classification, use them to inform timely response capacity, not as a direct estimate of all overdose events or of residents' risk.

## Demographics with harm-reduction implications

Harris County's population context makes language-accessible, low-cost delivery material to overdose prevention. U.S. Census Bureau QuickFacts, using 2020-2024 ACS estimates, reports 46.1% Hispanic or Latino, 20.5% Black alone, 24.9% non-Hispanic White alone, and 45.0% speaking a language other than English at home; categories overlap because Hispanic origin can be of any race. The same source reports 16.7% of residents below poverty and 22.8% of people under 65 without health insurance. These are **published estimates**, not overdose rates and not proof that any group is more likely to experience overdose. They do show why a Houston naloxone and treatment system should offer multilingual information, free or low-cost access, and routes that do not depend on insurance or a clinic visit; local subgroup mortality and program-reach data are needed before claiming who bears the greatest overdose burden.

Houston State of Health reports 2024 diagnosed HIV prevalence of 751.9 per 100,000 in Harris County, compared with 441.2 in Texas and 395.5 nationally; the county numerator is 30,878 and denominator 4,106,789, with a source update of 2026-06-18. The same published indicator reports 1,737.6 per 100,000 among Black residents, compared with 612.1 among Hispanic/Latino residents and 353.1 among White residents. HIV is not an overdose measure, and these rates do not establish injection as the transmission route for any person. They do show a local infectious-disease disparity relevant to service design: trusted, culturally responsive harm-reduction partners should connect people to confidential HIV prevention, testing, and care while county overdose subgroup rates and program reach are measured separately.

## Treatment capacity and infectious disease

Harris County has treatment and recovery programs, but the public inventory does not yet measure capacity per person in need or retention. SAMHSA's FindTreatment.gov lists facilities and updates annually, with monthly additions; it does not establish open appointments, medication slots, wait times, or retention. Harris County's ARPA-funded substance-use program names Cenikor, the Council on Recovery, Houston Recovery Center, and Santa Maria Hostel/Baylor as delivery partners for MAT, peer support, recovery housing, screening/referral, naloxone, and integrated care. These are a local service backbone, but the county page gives targets rather than a retention denominator. Confirm current services and hours with providers and obtain aggregate participant follow-up before claiming countywide capacity or retention.

A Houston-specific retention result exists for one post-overdose intervention, not for all treatment in Harris County. The Houston Emergency Opioid Engagement System (HEROES) pairs paramedics and peer recovery coaches, offers ED buprenorphine induction, and links survivors to outpatient treatment; the Brandeis Opioid Resource Connector reports that 88% of participants remained active in treatment after 30 days in a cited program evaluation. That result is a **published program outcome** from a selected post-overdose cohort, not a population-wide retention rate or proof of long-term retention. It shows that a Houston hospital/EMS entry point can connect some overdose survivors to early treatment, while leaving unanswered how many eligible survivors are contacted, decline, cannot be located, or remain after 30 days. Preserve that distinction in interpretation and request a current, de-identified program flow and follow-up summary before comparing HEROES with other county services.

Hepatitis C is central to the injection-related risk question, but no current Harris County HCV estimate by transmission category was established. Texas DSHS offers county/state surveillance and accepts hepatitis data requests; its resource report warns HCV care information is incomplete and treatment access may depend on indigent-care systems. That older statewide funding snapshot is not used as local prevalence. Ask HCPH/Houston Health and DSHS for privacy-protected county incidence, testing, and linkage measures. Meanwhile, Houston's integrated health and SUD services can offer HCV testing/referral alongside naloxone and MOUD without inferring that every person with HIV or HCV injects drugs.

## Criminal-justice intersection

Houston's overdose-response environment combines explicit emergency-call protections with criminal penalties that remain relevant outside their limited scope. Texas Health and Safety Code §481.115(g)-(i) provides a defense to prosecution only for specified low-quantity possession cases when the overdose caller meets conditions such as being first to request help, remaining at the scene, and cooperating; exclusions include an active arrest/search warrant, another offense, and prior use of the defense within 18 months, and the evidence collected can still be used for other offenses. Section 481.125 similarly provides a defense for simple paraphernalia possession with exclusions, while §481.125(a) otherwise makes that conduct a Class C misdemeanor. These are statutory rules, not measured Houston police practice. For a person deciding whether to call 911, the difference between a narrow defense and broad immunity may matter, particularly where more serious possession or other charges are possible. Houston/Harris County should measure bystander-call patterns and document local field practice rather than assume that statutory protection guarantees trust.

Harris County's current program design acknowledges both justice-connected access and the need for treatment linkage. The county's ARPA substance-use initiative lists specialty-court and judicial partners among outreach settings, while HEROES has a published justice-referral evaluation and describes coordinated care with first responders and community organizations. This creates a route to reach some people already involved with the courts, but it does not show how many are offered naloxone or MOUD, whether referrals are voluntary, or whether people outside justice settings are reached. Any local criminal-justice assessment should distinguish diversion and re-entry services from enforcement, and evaluate whether a justice referral is a bridge to care or a gate that excludes people not under supervision. The immediate implication is to include voluntary, non-custodial naloxone and treatment linkage in re-entry and specialty-court pathways while tracking coverage and exclusions.

## Existing harm-reduction and treatment infrastructure

Harris County has several named programs with distinct functions rather than one unified countywide naloxone system. HCPH operates the Substance Use Data Hub and county ARPA funding backs prevention, screening/referral, naloxone education/distribution, care coordination, MAT, and recovery housing through its four listed subrecipients. Houston Recovery Center operates the city's sobering center and, under the county-funded program, distributes naloxone and provides care coordination and recovery-housing linkage. The Council on Recovery offers Outreach, Screening, Assessment, and Referral and overdose-prevention/naloxone training; Cenikor expands MAT, peer support, and recovery housing; Santa Maria Hostel/Baylor operates an integrated clinic model for physical health, psychiatric care, and SUD treatment. These services create multiple clinical, peer, shelter, court, and community-event entry points, but the county's public description lists outreach channels rather than verified service-area coverage or hours. The planning implication is to inventory each organization's actual hours, eligibility, naloxone stock, languages, and referral completion before counting it as accessible supply.

HEROES supplies a medically anchored post-overdose pathway, while community organizations offer different entry points. The program connects EMS/ED survivors to peer coaching, ED buprenorphine, and outpatient MOUD; Brandeis' Opioid Resource Connector reports a 2025 Young HERO pilot intended to extend overdose education and naloxone training to youth. Texas law permits standing-order antagonist distribution and possession under Health and Safety Code §§483.102-483.106. Houston Harm Reduction Alliance lists one-hour naloxone training by appointment, with price varying by distance, illustrating both a community channel and a potential cost/logistics barrier. These channels can reach people through EMS, community organizations, or pharmacies, but no dated, deduplicated county access-point registry is in the project files; actual-hours verification is needed before claiming countywide coverage.

## Who is missed

Houston's most supportable missed-population hypothesis is people who use drugs but are not visible in named outreach settings, especially housed, working, socially connected people who do not identify as needing “drug treatment.” This is a **reach hypothesis, not a measured count**: Harris County lists community centers, assistance ministries, shelters, judicial partners, and specialty courts, alongside websites, social media, events, and partner referrals. These routes may miss people whose use is private and whose first contact is not an ED, shelter, or court. Houston needs discreet naloxone access in everyday settings and a privacy-protected reach audit comparing contacts by housing, employment, age, and referral source.

Houston's Young HERO pilot targets youth, but neither it nor the broader county outreach list establishes which age groups are reached at population scale. Brandeis' program profile describes the youth naloxone-education pilot, while HCPH publishes mortality views by age, sex, race, and substance; the project does not yet have matching, year-specific program-contact data. Without those denominators, naming a priority population is not evidence of adequate coverage. Houston partners should report aggregate trainings, naloxone distribution, referrals, and follow-up by suitable age group and geography, suppress small cells, and assess whether youth and other groups outside provider-connected settings are reached.

People who use stimulants or other non-opioid-labelled drugs, people experiencing overdose alone, and people reluctant to call emergency services are also plausible missed groups in Houston. The local trigger-based HEROES model starts with an EMS naloxone reversal, so it can offer strong follow-up to a survivor reached by EMS but cannot, by design, identify every event where no call was made or naloxone was unavailable. HCPH's ED/urgent-care ESSENCE feed captures visits but not people who never reach a facility, and its overdose-call protection is narrower than full immunity. These are documented boundaries of local data and program pathways, not proof of specific demographic exclusion. Houston should pair EMS/hospital-linked care with anonymous, low-threshold distribution and community reporting so that people outside clinical, judicial, shelter, and encampment outreach channels can access naloxone without first disclosing identity or accepting treatment.

## Single most critical structural gap

**Houston's single most consequential gap is the absence of a current, verified, countywide measure of who can actually obtain naloxone and who is reached by outreach, particularly outside EMS, shelter, court, and self-referral pathways, leaving planners unable to distinguish nominal program availability from equitable real-world coverage.**
