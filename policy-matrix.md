# Deliverable 2: Houston/Harris County Policy Matrix

**Assessment date:** 2026-10-17. This is the first half of the required eight-area matrix. “Real-world impact” separates what the law authorizes or penalizes from what local implementation data actually show. No Harris County enforcement, pharmacy-fill, 911-calling, or distribution dataset has yet been obtained; those impacts are not inferred from statutory text.

| **Naloxone standing orders, pharmacy dispensing, third-party access, and OTC availability.** Texas Health & Safety Code §§483.102-483.106 permits a prescriber to issue an opioid-antagonist prescription directly or by standing order to a person at risk or someone positioned to help; pharmacists may dispense; organizations under a standing order may store/distribute without compensation; any person may possess; and good-faith administration is protected. FDA approved OTC naloxone nasal spray in 2023. Sources: [Texas Chapter 483](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/HS/htm/HS.483.htm); [FDA OTC approval](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-over-counter-naloxone-nasal-spray). | State and federal; county/city implementation | **Active (2026-10-17).** Statutory pathways are active; no countywide dated access-point registry, operating-hours inventory, pharmacy-fill measure, or confirmed local standing-order implementation list is in the project files. | Law on paper creates multiple access routes but does not guarantee stock, affordability, open hours, language access, or trusted distribution in Houston. The current local evidence base identifies county-funded naloxone activities and public maps, but the underlying dated access list is unverified. Reach and exclusion therefore remain unmeasured. | The law permits broad community distribution and possession. Prioritize a verified, low-threshold outlet inventory, free peer distribution, and routine placement in nonclinical settings; do not treat statutory permission or an undated map as proof of practical access. |
| **Good Samaritan / emergency overdose-call protection.** Texas Health & Safety Code §481.115(g)-(i) provides a defense to prosecution for specified low-quantity possession when the caller is first to seek emergency assistance, remains, and cooperates, or when the person is the overdose victim. Related provisions cover certain low-level offenses in §§481.1151(c), 481.116(f), 481.1161(c), 481.117(f), 481.118(f), and paraphernalia possession in §481.125(g). Exclusions include active arrest/search activity, another offense, prior conviction/deferral, repeat prior use, and a recent prior emergency call; evidence related to offenses outside the defense may still be used. Source: [Texas Health & Safety Code Chapter 481](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/HS/htm/HS.481.htm). | State; applicable in Houston and Harris County | **Partial (2026-10-17).** A limited statutory defense is active, but it is not broad immunity and is restricted by offense, conduct, and exclusions. | The statute's narrow scope may leave callers uncertain about exposure when a case involves more than a qualifying low-level offense. No local survey, call data, or agency protocol was located showing whether Houston residents know the defense or whether it changes 911 behavior. Avoid claiming it either deters or increases calls without local evidence. | Community naloxone distribution and training remain legally available; messaging should accurately say the defense is limited and should not promise immunity. Pair distribution with clear overdose-response training and advocate for broader, comprehensible protections while collecting privacy-safe local evidence on emergency response. |
| **Fentanyl test strips / drug-checking equipment.** Texas Health & Safety Code §481.002(17)(D) includes testing equipment used or intended to test/analyze a controlled substance in the statutory definition of drug paraphernalia when tied to a Chapter 481 violation; §481.125(a)-(b) addresses possession and delivery of paraphernalia for prohibited uses. These provisions do not, by themselves, establish that every fentanyl test strip or every harm-reduction use is unlawful. No categorical FTS exemption was identified in the statutory text reviewed; bill history and controlling interpretation remain to be checked. Source: [Texas Health & Safety Code Chapter 481](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/HS/htm/HS.481.htm). | State; local distribution/enforcement may involve city and county actors | **Partial / legal ambiguity (2026-10-17).** Existing paraphernalia language is active; specific FTS exemption and current legislative status require a targeted bill-history and legal review. | Broad testing-equipment wording can create perceived or actual legal risk for people and organizations considering drug-checking. Whether Houston law enforcement has acted on FTS possession, and how programs currently distribute strips, is not established by this audit. Distinguish legal exposure from deterrence hypotheses. | This ambiguity does not block naloxone distribution under Chapter 483, but may constrain an integrated harm-reduction package. Until counsel confirms the rule, do not describe FTS as clearly legal or clearly prohibited; seek written guidance, document local program practice, and prioritize the independently authorized naloxone pathway. |
| **Possession penalties and paraphernalia enforcement.** Texas Health & Safety Code §§481.115, 481.1151, 481.116, 481.1161 and related provisions criminalize possession by penalty group/quantity; §481.125(a) makes specified simple paraphernalia possession a Class C misdemeanor. Statutory severity varies by substance and amount. Apart from opioid antagonists expressly addressed in Chapter 483, there is no general exemption for people carrying harm-reduction supplies. Source: [Texas Health & Safety Code Chapters 481 and 483](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/HS/htm/HS.481.htm). | State; enforced by state and local law-enforcement/courts | **Active (2026-10-17).** Penal provisions are active; project has not audited local charging, arrest, diversion, or paraphernalia-enforcement practice. | Criminal penalties can intersect with willingness to carry supplies, seek help, or disclose drug use, but the size and distribution of that effect in Houston are not measured here. Local arrest/charge records and community interviews would be needed; statutory exposure alone cannot establish actual deterrence or unequal enforcement. | Naloxone possession and good-faith administration have distinct statutory protections in Chapter 483 and should be clearly separated from controlled-substance/paraphernalia exposure. Community programs should use low-barrier distribution, avoid unnecessary identity collection, and pair policy advocacy with local implementation evidence. |

## Remaining required areas

These four areas are not yet assessed to the guide's standard and are not represented as completed: (5) syringe-service authorization, including the Harris County carve-out/pilot; (6) medications for opioid use disorder, including methadone rules and buprenorphine after elimination of the federal DATA waiver; (7) Medicaid expansion and OUD-treatment access; and (8) opioid-settlement allocations, including harm-reduction, treatment, and uncommitted shares. Each requires its own current-law and Houston/Harris County implementation review before the matrix is complete.

## Source and method note

Texas statute pages are current through the 89th Legislature's 2025 second called session, as stated on the official Texas Legislature site and accessed for this draft on 2026-10-17. Statutory citations describe law on the books, not observed enforcement. The remaining local-impact evidence needed includes dated county/city distribution and access records, public 911/EMS aggregates where privacy-safe, and disaggregated enforcement or diversion data. No individual records will be requested or retained.
